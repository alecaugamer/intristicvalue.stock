About this project

This is a self-initiated portfolio project built during my first year of studying Finance, Banking & Investment at EUBA (Ekonomická univerzita v Bratislave). The goal was to implement the four most widely used equity valuation models in a clean, interactive format — reinforcing theoretical knowledge with hands-on application.

Each model was designed to mirror real analyst workflows: using historical financial data (sourced from public 10-K filings), projecting forward estimates, and stress-testing assumptions through sensitivity analysis.

Valuation models

01 — DCF
Discounted Cash Flow
Multi-stage FCF growth, WACC via CAPM, terminal value, sensitivity vs WACC × TGR. Backtested on GOOGL.
02 — EV/EBITDA
Enterprise Value Multiple
Relative valuation using sector multiples, bear/base/bull case, peer group comparison (GOOGL, META, MSFT, AMZN).
03 — DDM
Dividend Discount Model
Two-stage DDM with high-growth phase transitioning to Gordon Growth terminal value. Backtested on JNJ.
04 — P/E + PEG
Earnings Multiple
Forward P/E implied price, PEG ratio analysis, peer group comparison across big tech.
05 — ARR / SaaS
Revenue Multiple Model
EV/ARR and EV/Revenue multiples for SaaS and recurring revenue businesses. NRR, churn, Rule of 40. Peer benchmarks.
06 — SOTP
Sum-of-the-Parts
Segment-by-segment valuation for tech conglomerates. Each business unit valued independently. Backtested on Alphabet.

  • Hybrid WACC — enter directly or calculate via CAPM components
  • Sensitivity analysis tables for every model
  • Peer group comparison with relative bar charts

methodology
How the models work

01 — DCF — Discounted Cash Flow

DCF is an intrinsic valuation method. The core idea: a company is worth the sum of all its future free cash flows, discounted back to today's value. It answers the question — if I own this business forever, what are all future cash flows worth right now?

multi-stage DCF formula
Intrinsic Value = Σ [ FCF_t / (1 + WACC)^t ] + Terminal Value / (1 + WACC)^n
Terminal Value = FCF_n × (1 + g) / (WACC − g)
Equity Value per Share = (Intrinsic Value − Net Debt) / Shares Outstanding

Key inputs explained

FCF
Free Cash Flow
Operating Cash Flow minus CapEx. The actual cash a business generates after maintaining/growing its assets. Not net income — FCF is harder to manipulate.
WACC
Weighted Avg. Cost of Capital
The discount rate — what return investors require. Calculated via CAPM for equity (Ke = Rf + β × ERP) combined with after-tax cost of debt, weighted by capital structure.
TGR
Terminal Growth Rate
The assumed perpetual growth rate after the projection period. Usually 2–3.5% — close to long-run GDP growth. Never above WACC, or the math breaks.
β
Beta
Measures systematic risk vs the market. Beta > 1 = more volatile than S&P 500. Used in CAPM to calculate cost of equity. JNJ has beta ~0.35, GOOGL ~1.11.

When to use DCF

  • Companies with stable, predictable free cash flows (mature tech, consumer staples)
  • When you want an absolute intrinsic value, not just relative to peers
  • Long-term fundamental investors (Warren Buffett style)

DCF limitations

  • Garbage in, garbage out — small changes in WACC or TGR create huge swings in output
  • Terminal value often represents 60–80% of total value — highly sensitive to assumptions
  • Useless for early-stage companies with no FCF history

02 — EV/EBITDA — Enterprise Value Multiple

A relative valuation method. Instead of projecting future cash flows, you ask: how much are comparable companies paying per dollar of EBITDA? Then apply that multiple to your target company.

EV/EBITDA formula
Enterprise Value (EV) = Market Cap + Net Debt (Total Debt − Cash)
EV/EBITDA Multiple = EV / EBITDA
Implied Share Price = (EBITDA × Fair Multiple − Net Debt) / Shares Outstanding

Why EV/EBITDA over P/E?

EV captures the full picture — equity + debt. EBITDA removes distortions from depreciation policies, interest rates, and tax rates. This makes it capital-structure-neutral — ideal for comparing companies with different leverage levels.

When to use EV/EBITDA

  • Comparing companies within the same sector (same industry = similar multiples make sense)
  • M&A analysis — acquirers look at EV because they take on debt too
  • Capital-intensive businesses where D&A is large (telecom, utilities, industrials)

Limitations

  • EBITDA ignores CapEx — misleading for very capital-intensive companies
  • Multiples are market-dependent: they compress during downturns and expand in bull markets
  • Comparing across sectors is meaningless — always compare within the same industry

03 — DDM — Dividend Discount Model

DDM values a stock as the present value of all future dividends. The two-stage version separates a high-growth phase from a stable terminal phase using Gordon Growth Model.

two-stage DDM
Phase 1 PV = Σ [ D_t / (1 + Ke)^t ] for t = 1 to n
Terminal Value = D_(n+1) / (Ke − g_terminal)
PV of TV = Terminal Value / (1 + Ke)^n
Intrinsic Value = Phase 1 PV + PV of Terminal Value

When to use DDM

  • Mature dividend-paying companies with stable payout history (utilities, REITs, Dividend Kings like JNJ)
  • Companies where dividends represent true shareholder returns (not just buybacks)
  • Income-oriented investment analysis

DDM is highly sensitive to Ke

The required return Ke (calculated via CAPM) has an outsized impact on the result. A 1% change in Ke can move the implied value by 20–30%. This is why the sensitivity table is so important — always stress-test your Ke assumption.

Limitations

  • Useless for non-dividend-paying companies (GOOGL paid no dividend until 2024)
  • Assumes dividends grow at a constant rate — may not hold in practice
  • Does not capture value from retained earnings reinvested into the business

04 — Forward P/E + PEG — Earnings Multiple

Forward P/E is the most widely quoted valuation metric on Wall Street. It answers: how much are investors paying per dollar of projected earnings? PEG ratio adds a growth dimension — adjusting the P/E for expected EPS growth rate.

P/E and PEG formulas
Trailing P/E = Current Price / TTM EPS
Forward P/E = Current Price / Next 12 Month EPS (analyst consensus)
Implied Price = Fair P/E × Forward EPS
PEG Ratio = Forward P/E / Expected EPS Growth Rate (%)
PEG < 1 → potentially undervalued | PEG > 2 → potentially expensive

Why Forward P/E beats Trailing P/E

Markets are forward-looking. Trailing P/E uses past earnings — but the stock price reflects what investors expect in the future. Forward P/E uses analyst consensus estimates for the next 12 months, which better captures expected profitability.

PEG ratio — the growth adjustment

Two companies can have the same P/E but vastly different growth rates. PEG normalizes for this. A company with P/E 30x growing at 30% per year (PEG = 1.0) may be cheaper than a company with P/E 15x growing at 5% per year (PEG = 3.0).

Limitations

  • EPS can be manipulated through buybacks, accounting choices, or one-off items
  • Forward EPS is based on analyst estimates — which are frequently wrong
  • Not suitable for loss-making companies, financials, or cyclicals with volatile earnings
framework
How to write a quality investment thesis

An investment thesis is a structured argument for why a specific stock is worth buying (or selling) at the current price. A good thesis is not "this company is great" — it's a specific, falsifiable claim backed by data. Sell-side analysts write these daily. Here is the framework professionals use.

Remember: A thesis is only as good as its ability to be proven wrong. Always define what would make you change your mind.

The 6-part thesis structure

01
Business overview — what does the company actually do?
Describe the business model in 2–3 sentences. Revenue streams, customers, competitive moat. Avoid copy-pasting the 10-K description — explain it in your own words. If you can't, you don't understand the business well enough yet.
02
The core thesis — your main bullish (or bearish) argument
One clear sentence: why is this stock mispriced? The market is wrong about X because of Y. Be specific. "GOOGL is undervalued because the market is not pricing in Google Cloud's path to 30%+ operating margins by 2027, which would add ~$40/share to intrinsic value."
03
Catalysts — what will close the gap between price and value?
Identify 2–3 specific events that could unlock value: earnings beats, margin expansion, new product launches, industry tailwinds, regulatory changes, buybacks. Without catalysts, even a correctly valued stock can stay cheap for years.
04
Valuation — what is the stock worth?
Use at least 2 methods (e.g. DCF + EV/EBITDA) and triangulate. Show a bear/base/bull case. State your price target and the time horizon. Never give just one number without context — always show the range of outcomes and the assumptions behind them.
05
Risks — what could go wrong?
This is where most students fail. Listing generic risks ("competition", "macro environment") is lazy. Think through specific, quantifiable risks: what happens to your DCF if WACC increases by 2%? What if Google loses its default search deal with Apple ($15–20B/year revenue)? Show you've stress-tested the thesis.
06
Variant view — why do you disagree with consensus?
What does the market think, and why are you right when they're wrong? This is the most important part for sell-side roles. If everyone already knows the thesis, it's already priced in. Your edge is the insight the consensus is missing.

Example thesis structure — GOOGL

illustration only — not investment advice

01 — business overview
Alphabet is the parent company of Google — the world's dominant search engine (90%+ market share), YouTube (2nd largest search engine globally), and Google Cloud (3rd largest cloud provider). ~77% of revenue comes from advertising, with Cloud growing at 30%+ YoY as the fastest-growing segment.
02 — core thesis
At a PEG ratio of ~0.84, GOOGL is priced as a mature, slow-growth company — yet EPS grew 34% YoY in 2025. The market is underpricing Google Cloud's margin expansion trajectory and the monetization optionality of Gemini AI embedded into Search, which has 1.5B monthly users on AI Overviews alone.
03 — catalysts
(1) Google Cloud crossing 15%+ operating margin — expected 2026. (2) AI Overviews driving Search revenue uplift as advertisers follow attention. (3) Continued buybacks reducing share count ~2% annually, boosting EPS mechanically.
04 — valuation
DCF base case (WACC 9%, TGR 3%, FCF growing ~15% for 6 years) implies $280–340/share. EV/EBITDA at historical median 18.6x on $155B forward EBITDA implies ~$260. Forward P/E at 25x on $13.50 EPS = $337. Weighted average target: ~$300, roughly in-line with current price — suggesting fair value with upside if Cloud margins expand faster than modeled.
05 — key risks
(1) DOJ antitrust ruling forcing divestiture of Chrome or Android — could eliminate $15–20B in default search revenue. (2) AI-native search (Perplexity, ChatGPT) eroding Search market share — every 1pp of market share lost ≈ ~$3B revenue. (3) Capex guidance of $175–185B in 2026 suppressing FCF if Cloud adoption disappoints.
06 — variant view
Consensus underestimates Google's AI monetization speed. Unlike OpenAI (which needs to build distribution), Google has the world's largest search distribution already. Gemini is not a startup competing for users — it's an upgrade to a product 5 billion people already use. The market prices Alphabet like an ad company at risk; the reality is it's an AI infrastructure company with an advertising moat subsidising R&D at scale.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Anchoring to a price target — build the model first, derive the target from that, not the other way around
  • Ignoring the balance sheet — a profitable company with unsustainable debt is a trap
  • One-method valuation — always triangulate at least 2 methods; if they diverge significantly, understand why
  • No time horizon — "this stock will go up" is not a thesis. "I expect X to reach Y within 18 months driven by Z" is
  • Not reading the 10-K — the footnotes contain what management doesn't want you to notice
